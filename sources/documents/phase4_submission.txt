Phase 4: Exhibition Text and Promotional Material · Team CakedUp


Overview
This document gives the final text for every panel, label and interactive in our pop-up
museum, followed by the promotional material we will use to bring visitors in. It follows
the floor plan and grouping from Phase 3: an entry panel, three groups (A Land and
Buildings, B Life on Campus, C Campus Systems), and a memory and exit station.

Writing rules we followed

       Length: group panels under 60 words; A3 panels under 120 words; object labels under
       30 words.
       Voice: each panel opens with a question or a surprising fact, then gives the answer in
       plain language. No jargon a first-year would not know.
       Voices of the campus: where we have them, we let staff and faculty speak in short
       quotes, credited by name. Every quote will be shown to the speaker before printing
       (see Phase 3, Permissions).
       Sources: each A3 panel ends with a one-line source note.

Changes since Phase 3

       Exhibition date: 14 November 2026.
       Where it began: first classes are now placed on the first two floors of Nilgiri, with SH-
       1 in Vindhya as the only large hall (Prof. P. J. Narayanan).
       New panels in Group A: the GDIT diploma and 2001 university status; the Vindhya
       corporate schools and the 2007 reclaim; the Himalaya complex, its 22 crore loan and T-
       Hub; Gachibowli from 6 to about 1,000 companies (Prof. K. S. Rajan).
       Group B rewritten: the housing story now runs from the Collector's bungalow (1998)
       to bunk beds in Vindhya (1999) to Palash, Parijat and Kadamb Nivas (2000). Added the
       2005 four-mess system and Yuktahar, and faculty and students living side by side until
       2010.
       Group C rewritten: the self-run Nilgiri server room and email, shared computers (1:3),
       paper files to IMS (around 2017), the substation on IIIT land, and a new panel on
       records, rules and the dual degree.
       New object: a blank government-style file folder from the office.

Entry panel (2 × 3 ft)
Do You Know Your Institute? The buildings, people and systems behind IIIT Hyderabad,
1998 to today

You walk past these buildings every day. But did you know that the first classes fit on two
floors of Nilgiri? That one batch slept on bunk beds inside Vindhya? That a corner of
Himalaya was once the state's startup incubator?

This exhibition tells the story of how IIIT-H was built and how it keeps running. Start with
the quiz at the table. Then walk left to right through three parts: Land and Buildings, Life
on Campus and Campus Systems. At the end, add your own memory and take the quiz
again.

Group A: Land and Buildings

Group panel (2 × 3 ft)
A. Land and Buildings How did two floors of one building become a 66-acre campus?

IIIT-H moved into buildings made for someone else, shared them with companies,
borrowed money to build more, and lent a building to the state. Follow how the campus
grew, one building at a time.


A1. It began on two floors of Nilgiri
On 2 September 1998, fifty students began classes in buildings put up for the Ranga
Reddy district Collectorate and courts. The state gave them to the new institute at a
nominal rent, and in about 100 days offices became classrooms and labs.

Only the first two floors of Nilgiri were in use. They held the classrooms, labs, offices and
exam halls. The only large hall was SH-1, next door in Vindhya.

There were just five or six faculty. Professors from the University of Hyderabad came in to
teach the basic sciences and humanities.

Sources: interview with Prof. P. J. Narayanan (2026); 25 Years of IIITH, IIIT blog (2023).


A2. A diploma on trust
The first batch did not join for a degree. They enrolled for a Graduate Diploma in
Information Technology (GDIT).

IIIT was not yet a university, so it could not award one. Students joined trusting that the
institute would get that status. It did in 2001, when IIIT-H became a deemed university.

The diploma left a trace online: students' early email addresses ended in @gdit.iiit.net,
while faculty used @iiit.net. Both later moved to iiit.ac.in.

Source: interview with Prof. P. J. Narayanan (2026).


A3. Companies in the classroom
In the late 1990s, Oracle, IBM, Motorola and Satyam ran their own schools inside Vindhya.
The idea was that company trainers would bring industry experience and make up for the
shortage of professors.

It did not work. The trainers taught company tools and basic Java, not the theory the
institute wanted. IIIT went back to faculty-led teaching, and in 2007 took full control of
Vindhya. The company rooms became research labs.

Look down in Vindhya blocks A1 and B2: the black granite floor and furniture were put in
by Motorola.

Some people stayed. Venu Gopal and Shekhar, two of the earliest staff, started at the IBM

A4. Himalaya: a loan, a startup hub and a buyback
To build the Himalaya complex, IIIT took a loan of 22 crore rupees, about 70 crore today. It
had ten years to repay it and finished in six.

The four blocks arrived as bare concrete shells, and each was finished differently:

A5. The Games next door
In 2003, Hyderabad hosted the 1st Afro-Asian Games at the stadium in Gachibowli, right
next to our campus. According to Mr. Gopi, the official press meets for the Games were
held in the IIIT guest house.

The Games also changed the map. About 30 acres of land were later given up for the
stadium's expansion.

Sources: interview with Mr. Gopi (2026); Engineering Office.


A6. From 6 companies to 1,000
What was outside the gate in 1998? Almost nothing.

Cyber Towers in HITEC City opened in 1998 with about six companies. By a 2007–08 study
for the state government, there were close to 1,000. In the 1990s, one district road from
Mehdipatnam was the only way to Gachibowli and the University of Hyderabad. Past
Tolichowki there was hardly a building until IIIT. By 2002 it had become a four-lane road.

The first shops nearby, a Reliance Fresh and a Spencer's, opened around 2007–08.

Group B: Life on Campus

Group panel (2 × 3 ft)
B. Life on Campus Where did students eat, sleep and spend their free time?

Forty-nine students arrived in 1998. Today there are thousands. Here is how the hostels,
messes and hangout spots changed to keep up, told by the people who were there.


B1. Bungalow, bunk beds, then hostels
Where does a new institute put its students?

       1998: the first batch lived in a District Collector's bungalow and three flats in the old
       court complex, where the new academic block is going up today.
       1999: with 99 of 100 seats filled, the new batch slept army-style on two-tier bunk beds
       inside Vindhya, where block A4 and the library are now.
       2000: with government help, Palash Nivas and Parijat Nivas opened, followed by
       Kadamb Nivas. The bunk beds came down.

Once the hostels opened, faculty moved into the bungalow and flats the students had
left.

Sources: interview with Prof. P. J. Narayanan (2026); interview with Mr. Gopi (2026).

B2. Faculty next door
For the first twelve years, there was no separate faculty housing. Faculty lived beside the
hostels and ate at the same mess tables as students.

One professor living near Kadamb says he followed televised cricket matches without a
TV. He just listened for the students' cheers across the courtyard.

Dedicated faculty quarters opened in 2010.

Source: interview with Prof. P. J. Narayanan (2026).


B3. From one mess to four
In 1998 there was exactly one mess, and before the hostels were ready the guest house
served as the dining hall. Mr. Gopi managed it. His unofficial duties included chasing away
wild boars, deer and buffaloes, which wandered in because the campus had no fence.

By 2005 IIIT had settled on a system of four messes, each with its own regional menu.
Students could register and cancel flexibly from the start.

The newest, Yuktahar, exists because students campaigned for clean, wholesome food
based on Ayurvedic principles.


    "[Short quote from Mr. Gopi on the early mess, approved by him]"

Sources: interviews with Mr. Gopi and Prof. P. J. Narayanan (2026); Meet the Unseen Pillars
of IIIT-H, IIIT blog (2018).

Group C: Campus Systems

Group panel (2 × 3 ft)
C. Campus Systems What keeps the campus running when no one is looking?

The servers, the computers, the paperwork, the water and the power. Each was built by
someone, and most of it used to work very differently.


C1. A network the institute ran itself
IIIT's first server room is in Nilgiri, and its servers are still running.

From the start, the institute wanted to own its systems. It bought bare server hardware
and ran its own email on Zimbra, instead of paying an outside company. Students worked
alongside IT staff as system administrators. Email later moved to Microsoft.

Now, after nearly 25 years in Nilgiri, the server room is moving to the IT wing in Vindhya.

[Photo: the Nilgiri server room, with permission from IT Services]

Sources: interview with Prof. P. J. Narayanan (2026); IT Services.


C2. One computer for three students
Could you study computer science without your own computer?

Early students did. B.Tech students shared workstations on the library's second floor,
three to a machine. M.Tech students shared one between five, and some used their own.
First-year students were not allowed to bring a personal computer at all.

The shared system was slowly replaced by three open-access workspaces, and the TL-1
lab opened before COVID.

Look at the hardware on Table 2. Can you guess what each one was used for? Turn the
label over to check.

Source: interview with Prof. P. J. Narayanan (2026).


C3. From paper files to IMS
Before portals, everything moved on paper.

The office ran on government-style cloth and card file folders, like the one on the table.
Staff carried news of class changes to students in person. Around 2017, work began
moving into IMS, one process at a time.

Most paperwork now runs through IMS. But some records are still kept on paper, because
official and audit rules require hard copies.

Object: Blank file folder of the kind used in the IIIT office before IMS. Most students today
have never seen one.

Sources: interview with Prof. K. S. Rajan (2026); Meet the Unseen Pillars of IIIT-H, IIIT blog
(2018).


C4. Water and power
Where does the campus get its power? Partly from its own land.

An electricity substation stands on IIIT land, leased to the power utility, and it is the
institute's main source of power. Water comes from the city water board (HMWSSB).

When the power fails, electricians Mallesh and Naidu, here since 2005 and 2006, get the
call. In 2007, construction workers near a hostel damaged the substation cable and a fire
broke out. Naidu shut off the main panel, and the team worked past midnight to restore
power.

Sources: interview with Prof. K. S. Rajan (2026); Meet the Unseen Pillars of IIIT-H, IIIT blog
(2018).

C5. Records, rules and how decisions get made
Which papers matter most to an institute's history? Not the grants or the power permits.
The Memorandum of Association that set up IIIT and the land papers are kept under lock
and key, and many now have digital copies.

Rules from bodies like UGC and AICTE are adopted as they are, and changes never apply
backwards: students partway through a programme are not made to redo courses.

One habit has lasted since 1998: decisions are made by consulting widely. Once everyone
sat in one room. Now it runs through committees and representatives.

Then and now: curriculum documents from before 2008, 2008 and 2018, side by side.

Source: interview with Prof. K. S. Rajan (2026); IIIT curriculum archive.


C6. The research bet: the dual degree
IIIT's first dual degree let B.Tech students switch from a four-year programme to a five-
year one with research built in. It was planned around 2000–01.

It set IIIT apart as a research university. Early on, many students converted. Today the
number is in single digits.
